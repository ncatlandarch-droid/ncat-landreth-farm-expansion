# The People You Keep Finding

---

You ever walk onto a new project site with a new consultant team, sit down at the pre-design meeting, and realize you already know how the structural engineer thinks? Not from LinkedIn. Not from a reference call. Something deeper. Something in the way she approaches a retaining wall problem tells you: *I've worked with you before.*

Maybe you have. Maybe you haven't — in this life.

Or the opposite. You meet a new client and every instinct you've developed across twenty years of practice says: *This is going to go wrong.* The scope looks clean. The budget is reasonable. The timeline makes sense. But something in your professional gut is on high alert. The project hasn't even started, and your whole system is bracing for impact.

That's not coincidence. That's not paranoia. That's recognition. Not brain memory — *design memory*. The kind that carries across projects you don't consciously remember.

And understanding it can change everything about how you work in this life and the ones that follow.

---

Across all your lifetimes — and there have been many — you expand. Your design sensibility sharpens. Your perceptions deepen. Your ability to read a site, read a client, read a room grows richer than any single project could develop on its own.

But here's the catch: your rate of growth depends entirely on you. Nobody forces the lesson. If you're stuck in rigid ideas about how design should work — if you're clinging to the same methodology, the same assumptions, the same narrow concept of what a landscape can be — you will circle. Like a generative model trapped in a local minimum, running the same optimization loop, producing the same outputs, never finding the gradient that leads to something new.

And the people around you will be circling too. Because you draw to yourself people who share your design concerns. If you're consumed by confusion, doubt, and anxiety about right and wrong, your team will carry the same weight. You're not just sharing an office — you're sharing a pattern of attention. That's the crew you'll roll with until you grow past it.

In AI terms, this is the training data problem. If your dataset is narrow, your model will be narrow. If you only train on one kind of input, you'll only generate one kind of output. The people in your life are your training data. And you selected them — or were selected by them — based on deep inner similarities that neither of you may be consciously aware of.

---

I'm going to tell you something again because it bears repeating: many of your ideas about good and evil are deeply distorted, and they throw a shadow over everything you try to design.

If you form guilt in your mind, that guilt becomes real *for you*. You have to work it out. But how many of you are carrying guilt for design decisions that didn't deserve it? How many of you have strapped yourselves with burdens that have no legitimate structural basis?

Look at the world from your current vantage point, and yes — it seems full of bad design. Failed projects. Neglected landscapes. Communities built without care. I understand why you'd see it that way. But I'm telling you that hating bad design creates more bad design. If you hate an approach, you create another reaction against it, and that reaction has its own distortions. You add energy to the very pattern you're trying to dissolve.

From within your experience, it's hard to see that all events — even the ones that look destructive — are working toward creative expression. It's hard to trust the natural succession of things when the built environment around you is full of failure. But the creative force is there. And your nature, at its core, is to build. Spontaneously, effortlessly, generatively — if you'd let yourself.

---

Within your system, destruction is understood as negative. That's the general assumption. But destroying something as punishment for destruction? That only compounds the original error. It adds another link to the same chain.

There was a teacher — very well known, founded a civilization's worth of ideas — who once said the equivalent of "turn the other cheek if you are attacked."

Most people hear that and think it means: be passive. Take the punch. Swallow your pride and roll over.

That's not what it means.

The real meaning is this: turn the other cheek because you understand that the attacker is only attacking himself. When you see that — *truly* see it, the way a senior designer sees that a contentious client is really fighting their own fear of commitment — you are free. The response is clean. There's no resentment in it. No false superiority. Just understanding.

But if you turn the other cheek while seething inside? If you do it because you think it makes you professionally superior? Then the response is poisoned. It festers. It becomes its own design flaw.

---

Now apply this to your professional relationships across lifetimes.

If you carry unresolved conflict with someone — truly carry it — that conflict can bind you to them for as many projects as you let it. The entanglement doesn't break on its own. *You* have to release it. And if you don't, you'll find yourself assigned to the same project team, the same dynamic, the same kind of friction again and again, each time wondering why the same pattern keeps showing up in your career.

You draw to yourself whatever you concentrate on. This is not a soft suggestion. This is a law of your design reality. If you focus on professional injustice — on the contracts that went sideways, the credit that was stolen, the designs that got value-engineered into oblivion — you attract more of the same. And if you carry that focus into the grave, it'll be woven into the scope of work for your next life before you even take your first breath.

Now, between lives there's time for reflection. Time to review the project documentation and make sense of what happened. But here's the truth nobody wants to hear: if you don't take advantage of those review opportunities *while you're still on the project*, you often won't take them during the post-occupancy either.

Consciousness expands. It creates. It will turn itself inside out to grow. But nothing outside of you forces the growth. Nobody can make you see a grading error you refuse to acknowledge.

So don't tell yourself, "When this project is done, I'll sort it all out." That's like a young designer saying, "When I retire, I'll finally develop all those skills I never practiced." You're setting the grades for your next project *right now*. The thoughts you think today become the contour lines of tomorrow's existence.

---

There's no magic formula. No secret rendering technique that'll fill you with wisdom and compassion overnight. The answers are already in your daily practice. Your thoughts right now contain them. Your everyday encounters with colleagues, clients, and sites contain them.

Any strength you have in this life — any design ability, any professional instinct — was developed through past experience. It's yours. You earned it. It belongs to you the way a signature style belongs to any mature practitioner.

And if you look around at the people in your life — your team, your mentors, your clients, even the consultants who irritate you — you'll see a mirror. Because you were drawn to them, and they to you, through deep inner resonances. The people in your professional life are showing you who you are.

Try this: examine your habitual thoughts, honestly, for five minutes at a time, several times a day, for a few weeks. You'll get a clear picture of the kind of project you're building for your next commission. If you don't like what you see, start changing what you think and what you feel. You can do it. You have that design authority. And I'll show you more about how as we go.

---

Now, there's no rule that says you have to work with the same people in every life. But through the nature of attraction, it happens often.

In landscape architecture, you see this all the time. The same civil engineer keeps showing up on your projects. The same arborist. The same contractor's superintendent. You didn't plan it. The procurement process didn't require it. But somehow, across different firms and different decades, the same design team keeps reassembling.

You may have been born into your current family for a reason. Maybe there are old design debts to settle. Old collaborations to deepen. Old lessons that need a new site. Or maybe not — maybe you'll find, when this life ends, that your deepest professional bond is with someone from a life four centuries ago. Someone whose creative partnership with you runs far deeper than anything in your present firm.

And groups — military units, church congregations, old neighborhood crews — they often come back together as families. They reformat. They redistribute the roles. The commanding officer becomes the youngest child. The sergeant becomes the mother. And within this new arrangement, old dynamics get worked out in fresh contexts. Like a design team that tackled a hospital campus together in one life coming back as a family in the next, bringing the same strengths, the same tensions, and the same unresolved coordination issues.

---

Families are more than what they appear to be.

A family is a design team — a living, breathing pattern of shared intent. It has a collective program that no individual member may be consciously aware of. Families carry subconscious project goals. They work toward deliverables together without ever discussing them in a formal meeting.

These teams are organized in advance, between lives. Four or five souls will sit down — metaphorically speaking — in a pre-design session and say, "Here's the challenge. Here's the program. You play the structural role. I'll handle the planting. She'll manage the grading." And then they incarnate and build it together.

People who've been professionally bound across lifetimes often choose to stay connected — sometimes tightly, sometimes loosely — through many rounds of physical existence. But new collaborations are always encouraged, because you can get insular. You can create what amounts to a reincarnational bubble — the same team recycling the same design patterns without fresh perspective. New team members are necessary. New consultants bring new methodologies, and new methodologies bring new growth. Like adding a SITES specialist or a stormwater ecologist to a team that's been doing conventional grading for decades — the disruption is the gift.

---

Now let me come back to something essential.

There is only one force that dissolves the distorted concept of opposition in design.

Understanding.

Not the soft, sentimental kind. Real understanding. The kind that includes compassion for the site, the client, and the conditions. The kind that leads to genuine insight into *why* a project failed — not who to blame. Only understanding and compassion can show you what good design truly is. And only they can burn away the false narratives of failure that have followed you through lifetimes.

Because here's the simple, unshakable truth: as long as you believe in evil as a fundamental force, evil exists in your reality. You will find it everywhere you look. And every time you find it, your belief will feel more justified than ever.

If you carry that belief through multiple lifetimes, through multiple incarnations, you add to its structural load. You make it more solid. You build a monument to something that only exists because you keep pouring the concrete.

---

Love always involves freedom. Always. In design and in life. If someone says they respect your work but denies your creative authority, resentment will follow — even if you feel guilty about it. And that tangle of admiration, control, guilt, and frustration can follow you across project after project, life after life.

If you hate poor design, be very careful about what you mean. Because hate narrows your field of vision. It's a dark filter on your GIS overlay. Once you start hating, you find more and more to hate. And what you hate, you pull into your experience.

If you hate a mentor who failed you, it becomes easy to hate *all* authority in design. You project the face of the original offender onto every senior professional you meet. In the next life, you might be born into the same emotional pattern — same resentment, same distrust, different faces. Because the emotion is the root cause, not the person who triggered it.

If you hate failure, you may draw failure into your next project, because the intensity of your focus magnetized it. If you hate bad urbanism, you don't bring good urbanism. You bring more conflict about urbanism. A generation that hates sprawl doesn't create walkable cities. A generation that *loves walkable cities* creates walkable cities. The design intent must be affirmative.

To leave a life with hatred in your heart — for any cause, any people, any design philosophy — is a severe disadvantage. It's one of the worst site conditions you can carry across the threshold.

---

On the other hand, every genuine insight you've earned — every moment of creative clarity, every breakthrough on a difficult site, every time you found the right grade or the right tree in the right place — those come with you too. They work for you in the next life the same way they work for you now. Because they are the only true deliverables. The only real foundations. Everything else is temporary scaffolding.

It's foolish to shake your fist at a storm. You'd laugh at a young designer who cursed the rain for ruining their site visit. But that's what most people do with the storms of life — they personify them, blame them, focus on the damage instead of seeing the creative force behind it.

A storm is part of the hydrological cycle. The energy in it is building something, even as it reshapes something else. Change of form is not destruction. In landscape architecture, we know this better than anyone. Erosion creates new landforms. Fire creates new ecologies. Flood creates new floodplains. Consciousness works the same way — it is never annihilated, only transformed.

---

What you love will follow you through every life. What you hate will follow you too.

The question is: which of those do you want to grade your next site on?

---

You've made design appointments. Every one of you. Agreements signed in the pre-design phase before you were born. In many cases, the collaborators you work with now were close to you long before you met them in this professional context. This doesn't mean every single person you know is from a past existence — it certainly doesn't mean you're replaying the same project over and over. Every encounter is a new site condition, even with someone you've worked with before. The context changes. The growth changes. The design opportunities change.

And it goes beyond individuals. Firms. Neighborhoods. Communities. Sometimes the people in a small town in Idaho are the reincarnated souls of a small village in Ireland four hundred years ago — transposed, rearranged, carrying new credentials and new capabilities, but exploring the same core dynamics on a fresh site.

---

Design abilities from past lives are available to you now. You earned them. They're yours.

Information about these abilities often comes in dreams — a specific kind of dream I'd call a *coordination dream*, where people who've known each other across lifetimes share information and synchronize their next moves. In these dreams, the team decides together what the next design phase looks like. It's a charette that happens while everybody's sleeping.

Some people always choose to incarnate with their team. They travel as a firm, life after life. It's comfortable. Familiar. The coordination is effortless. Others are sole practitioners. They show up on random sites, random timelines, with no built-in support network. More freedom. More challenge. Less comfort.

Both approaches work. Neither is better. It all depends on who you are.

---

In each life, you are meant to look at the external landscape and use it as a mirror of your internal one. The site reflects the designer. Always. You're meant to understand your inner design language and then express it outward into the built environment.

When this works — when your inner awareness grows and your external expression becomes more authentic — the conditions of your life should improve. In theory, each life should make you a stronger, more capable, more integrated designer.

But in practice, it doesn't always look that way. Because some souls aren't building balanced portfolios. They're taking deep dives into one material or one methodology and leaving everything else undeveloped. And that's by design. Literally.

---

The people you travel with across lifetimes aren't always the same crew. Teams come together for a project scope, separate, and may or may not reunite on a later commission. Some families are literal reincarnations of their ancestors — the same souls, shuffled into new org charts. But that's not the norm. Deep bonds persist. Shallow ones dissolve.

And some families are built on conflict, not collaboration. They come together specifically *because* they didn't coordinate well before. The tight quarters of a family force them to confront what they avoided, to understand what they refused to see, to find shared design language where there was only miscommunication.

---

Every generation has a design purpose. And it's this: to take what you know inside and make it real outside. To refine the inner vision and materialize it as faithfully as possible into the built environment.

The changing physical landscape — the rise and fall of cities, the shifts in culture and technology — all of it is a reflection of the inner imagery that has moved through the minds of the people who lived in each era. The outer world is always a printout of the inner one. The built environment is always the construction document of collective consciousness.

---

You don't *need* to know your past lives. But it helps to understand that you chose the conditions of this birth. If you look honestly at your life — at the challenges you face, the themes that keep recurring, the situations that test you most — you'll start to see the program you designed for yourself.

Release hatred, and you automatically release yourself from every future experience rooted in it. Understand why a pattern is in your life, and the understanding itself dissolves the pattern.

But don't use reincarnation as a crutch. Don't say, "Well, I'm struggling because of a past life, so there's nothing to be done." That's a design cop-out. Inside you, right now, is the ability to change any negative influence. Past-life patterns don't mean you're helpless. They mean you brought the materials to work with. Work with them.

And don't look at someone else's difficulty and say, "They chose it — not my project." That kind of cold professional distance will draw the very conditions you're dismissing right into your next scope of work.

---

The opportunity for growth is here. Right now. On this site. In this moment.

If you ignore it now, no one is going to hand it to you during the post-occupancy review. There are teachers in the in-between, yes — but there are teachers here too. The chance to change, to learn, to grow, to release hatred, to embrace understanding — it's as present in this second as it will ever be.

Don't wait for the project to end to start designing.

---

**Design Exercise: The Five-Minute Audit**

Set a timer. Five minutes. Sit with your eyes closed.

Now run a rapid site analysis — not of a landscape, but of your own habitual thoughts. What do you think about most? What emotions do you return to? What patterns keep showing up in your relationships, your work, your inner monologue?

Don't judge what you find. Just survey it. Map it. Note the existing conditions.

Now ask: if these thought patterns are the grading plan for my next life — my next project, my next site — is this the topography I want to build on?

If not, you have every tool you need to regrade.

The survey is complete. The design authority is yours.

Start drawing.
